# Round 1: opening positions

## Graham

SEAT: Graham (Product and startups)
STANCE: Yes
OPTION SCORES: A 8/10 · B 4/10 · C 5/10 · D 2/10
PILAR SCORE: 6/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: A, but run it by hand: be the booking cockpit yourself for 3 to 5 hauliers before you write the parser, and charge £50 from week two.

WHY:
1. Small intense fire (S098). The test is "Who wants this so much that they'll use it even when it's a crappy version one made by a two-person startup they've never heard of?" (S098) A small haulier chasing three operators by email might. A ferry operator putting IMDG segregation checks in a stranger's side project will not. B fails this test on day one.
2. Do things that don't scale (S096). Hauliers let you deliver the product by hand. You forward their requests, read the replies and send one status sheet. "The unscalable things you have to do to get started are not merely a necessary evil, but change the company permanently for the better." (S096) You can't hand-run B. It needs an adapter, rules validation and procurement first.
3. The numbers, with my assumptions stated. The £15k target is about £1,250 a month. At an assumed £100 average that is 13 paying hauliers by month twelve, which is slow, steady growth from 2 pilots. One B operator at £2,000 gets there faster on paper. But that cheque comes from an enterprise sale, run part time, against your own employer's industry. That is "broad but hypothetical", and "A need that's narrow but genuine is a better starting point than one that's broad but hypothetical." (S174) C is too slow: letters of intent are a waitlist, not usage. A, done by hand, already includes C's conversations.

THE RISK I SEE: Hauliers say the pain is real but won't pay £50, because their freight forwarder or one favourite operator already covers it. Then you have a polite niche that never grows.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Fewer than 3 of the first 10 hauliers paying after a hand-run fortnight. Or a non-employer operator offering a paid pilot of at least £2,000 a month within 60 days.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Phone 10 small hauliers you already know and offer to handle next week's ro-ro bookings for free, by hand.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Can you name five hauliers who have complained to you this month about chasing booking confirmations?

## Bezos

SEAT: Bezos (Strategy and decisions)
STANCE: Yes
OPTION SCORES: A 8/10 · B 4/10 · C 5/10 · D 2/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: A, hauliers first and sell before you build, after an employment lawyer has cleared the IP and outside-business clauses.

WHY:
1. Sort the door first (Type 1 and Type 2, S063). A is a two-way door. It costs £50 a month to try, the pilots are free, and you can walk back out. B looks reversible but it isn't. You would be selling to your own employer's competitors, and hazardous-goods validation is a safety matter, so one IMDG miss is a reputation hit you don't come back from. "Type 2 decisions can and should be made quickly by high judgment individuals or small groups." (S063)
2. Work backwards from the customer and stay narrow. What a haulier will still want in ten years: fewer emails, faster confirmations, one place to see status. That is a need that doesn't change. "be as narrow and as focused as is possible to be" (S018). B's bigger cheque is real. One operator at £2,000 to £5,000 a month covers the £15k to £20k target by itself. I'm assuming a 6 to 12 month operator sales cycle, which runs past your six-month clock. You'd need about 12 hauliers at an average of £100 a month, and that is an input you control.
3. Spend early effort removing the biggest risk. "What startup companies do is they take their precious early capital dollars and systematically eliminate risks." (S008) The top risk is demand, not code. C removes it too, but it ships nothing, and the endorsement needs a working product. D is the acts-of-omission trap: "Most regrets, by the way, are acts of omission and not commission." (S043)

THE RISK I SEE: The employment contract turns out to give your employer the IP, so the whole thing becomes a one-way door before you have made a single sale.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Two operators signing letters of intent at £2,000 a month or more within 60 days, with the employer conflict cleared in writing. Then B moves up.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Get the contract reviewed, then book the first five haulier conversations.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Does your contract let you sell anything to ro-ro operators or hauliers while you're employed, and have you seen that in writing?

## Hormozi

SEAT: Hormozi (Offers, pricing, and sales)
STANCE: Conditional yes (on B, sold before built)
OPTION SCORES: A 4/10 · B 7/10 · C 5/10 · D 2/10
PILLAR SCORE: 7/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: B, sold before built: pre-sell a paid planner-assistant pilot to one operator that is not your employer, and write no product code until a cheque clears.

WHY:
1. LTV to CAC (S070, S089). My assumptions: £3,000/mo, 80% gross margin, 24-month life, so lifetime gross profit is about £57,600. CAC is about £5,000 of founder time and travel, which gives about 11:1. A at £200/mo with SMB churn of 5%/mo gives about £3,400 LTGP. At £50/mo it's about £850, and that tier dies on CAC alone. "SMBs suck as customers." (S028)
2. Math to target. £15k to £20k needs 7 to 8 hauliers on £200 all year, or 25+ on £50. One operator at £2,000/mo hits it. "I'd rather spend $100 and make $1,100 than spend $1 to make $2." (S012)
3. Value Equation (S115). A desk of 6 planners costs about £240k a year loaded (assumed £40k each). If you take 30% of the email grind off them, that's £72k of value against a £36k price. That is a 2:1 offer the buyer can check on a napkin. Your domain edge raises perceived likelihood, which A doesn't use.
4. Client-financed acquisition (S037). Charge a £5,000 setup fee for the pilot. "30-day cash must be greater than two times the cost of acquiring customer and the cost of fulfilling that customer." On the £5k CAC I assumed in point 1, a £5k fee covers one CAC, not two, so make the first invoice the setup fee plus the first month (£8,000 at £3,000/mo). Don't run free pilots. Trade a lower price for a written statement and a case study, because "I personally never discount unless there's a change in terms" (S033).
5. C without an ask is weak. Letters of intent cost the buyer nothing. Ask for money in every conversation.

THE RISK I SEE: The employer conflict. Selling to your employer's competitors could cost you the job and the IP before you have revenue.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Fewer than 3 of the first 10 operator conversations agreeing to a paid pilot. Or a sales cycle longer than 4 months, which misses the six-month endorsement window. Either one moves me to A at £200 only.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Get the employment contract's outside-business and IP clauses reviewed. Then write a one-page paid-pilot offer: price, setup fee, scope, and a human sign-off on every IMDG flag.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: Name five operators, outside your employer, whose route-desk head would take your call this month. Which of them would pay £5,000 up front?

## Buffett

SEAT: Buffett (Money and capital)
STANCE: Conditional yes (on C, done as the first step toward A)
OPTION SCORES: A 6/10 · B 3/10 · C 8/10 · D 4/10
PILLAR SCORE: 8/10
CONFIDENCE: 4/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: C with a deadline. Get the employer's outside-business and IP clauses cleared first, then spend 8 weeks collecting written statements from hauliers, and turn the best one into a free A pilot by month four.

WHY:
1. Rule 1, never risk ruin (S095): "A string of wonderful numbers times zero will always equal zero." The cash here is about £3,000, which is small and capped. The thing that could take you to zero is the salary. I'm assuming about £35k a year. Option B means selling a planner tool to your employer's direct competitors, built on what you learned at their route desk. One IP claim could cost you the job and the business together. That sets B to zero for me, whatever the £2,000 to £5,000 cheques say.
2. Birds in the bush (S011): "how much do you get back, how sure are you of getting it, when do you get it?" On A, the £15k year-one target means about 25 hauliers at £50, or 6 to 7 at £200. That's a lot of small birds, but each one is cheap to catch and none of them is your employer's rival. On C, the birds cost nothing, because you're only spending evenings and conversations.
3. Reputation beats money (S028): "We can afford to lose money but we can't afford to lose reputation." A wrong IMDG segregation call is a safety failure that makes the front page, and B is the option that makes that call. A only parses replies and shows status, so a mistake there is just a missed email. My lens misfires when someone is building from zero (S051), and with this little money at stake I won't ask for Berkshire-style patience. Inactivity is not the answer, so D loses.

THE RISK I SEE: The employer finds out before the clauses are reviewed and calls it moonlighting against their own customers. Then the job, and possibly the visa plan built around it, goes before any revenue comes in.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: Written employer consent that names operator-side software. With that and one operator LOI at £2,000 a month or more, B goes to 7.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Pay an employment solicitor about £300 (my assumption) to read the contract's IP and outside-business clauses before any conversation happens.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: If your employer read every message you've sent to a prospect, would you still have a job on Monday?

## Martell

SEAT: Martell (People, hiring, and founder time)
STANCE: Conditional yes (on A)
OPTION SCORES: A 7/10 · B 4/10 · C 6/10 · D 3/10
PILLAR SCORE: 6/10
CONFIDENCE: 3/5
RUIN RISK: possible
DOOR: two-way

THE CALL: Go with A, but run the first four weeks as pure C-style selling and write no code until 2 hauliers say yes to a pilot.

WHY:
1. Perfect Week (S039). 20 hours a week for 26 weeks is about 520 founder hours, and there are no other hours. My assumption: B's rules engine (IMDG segregation, lane metres, reefer plugs, adapter) eats 300+ of them before a single operator signs, and enterprise procurement eats the rest. A leaves room to sell. I'd split the week: 10 hours selling, 8 building, 2 admin. That's a big rock you lock in before the day job fills it.
2. Founder-led sales, Replacement Ladder rung four (S031, S020). You're pre-revenue, so your Buyback Rate is close to zero. Nothing gets delegated. You do the conversations by hand. "Most Founders should keep those conversation." [S020, 2023] Hauliers pick up the phone and decide fast. Operators work in committees. Committees burn calendar.
3. Focus (S030, S099). "In the early days is you pick a niche, and you go deep." [S099, 2016] B also puts you selling to your employer's competitors. That's a conflict you'd be managing every week on top of a full-time job. Pick one side, one offer, one channel.

THE RISK I SEE: Support load. Hitting £15k a year at £50 a month means about 25 hauliers. Each one emails you at 7 a.m. while you're at your desk at the ferry operator. My assumption is 1 hour a week per customer, which is 25 hours. That's more than your whole week.

WHAT WOULD CHANGE MY MIND: If one operator signs a letter of intent at £2,000 a month or more inside 60 days, B wins. One customer covering the target costs far less of your calendar than 25.

WHAT I'D DO THIS WEEK: Block two 5-hour slots on the calendar and book 10 haulier calls inside them. Also get the employment contract's IP and outside-business clauses read before anything else goes ahead.

QUESTION FOR THE FOUNDER: If a pilot customer needs you at 10 a.m. on a Tuesday, what exactly do you do, and who covers it?
